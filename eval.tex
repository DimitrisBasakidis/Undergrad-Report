\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Lucene Evaluation}

We begin by quantifying the performance impact of static and dynamic DRAM partitioning under varying budgets. 
For both the M1 and M2 workloads, we collect metrics including total execution time, 
young GC time, mixed GC time.

Figures~\ref{fig:m1-exec} and~\ref{fig:m2-exec} summarize the normalized execution time breakdown for both workloads.
Each DRAM setting is shown as a pair of bars: the left bar corresponds to the static TeraHeap~(TH) configuration, 
and the right to the dynamic FlexHeap~(FH).

Each bar is split into three regions: 
light gray indicates non-GC execution time, 
blue represents young GC time, 
and orange captures mixed GC time.

In both benchmarks, FlexHeap's dynamic partitioning consistently outperforms the static used by TeraHeap, particularly under smaller memory budgets.  
In M1's benchmarks, for the 3 largest DRAM configurations we observe an average speedup of 7.6\%. An outlier is the 8\,Gb DRAM configuration
where we notice a speedup of 70\%. As discussed earlier in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, due to contrained H1 DRAM budget, the static partitioning configuration 
fails to allocate sufficient space to the young generation, resulting in up to 8$\times$ higher time 
spent in young GC collection. 

M2 also benefits from FlexHeap, with execution times reduced 
by 4.7\%, 3.5\%, 2.9\%, and 25.4\% at 40\,Gb, 20\,Gb, 10\,Gb and 8\,Gb accordingly. 


FlexHeap also reduces read traffic on the H2 file device.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:m1-traffic}, FlexHeap lowers read traffic for M1 by 93.6\%, 
91.1\%, and 65.7\% at 20\,Gb, 10\,Gb, and 8\,Gb DRAM, respectively. At 40\,Gb DRAM, read traffic increases 
by 117.4\% but, still reports faster execution due to better tail 
latency (99th percentile latency improves from 1350\,ms to 1327\,ms) and higher throughput (Queries Per Second).

For M2, as presented in Table~\ref{tab:m2-traffic}, FlexHeap reduces read traffic consistently across all DRAM configurations by 57.0\% at 40\,Gb, 
92.2\% at 20\,Gb, 88.9\% at 10\,G, and 71.5\% at 8\,Gb while also improving GC and execution time with better latency and higher QPS.

Figure~\ref{fig:m2-iow-cpu} provides a visual breakdown of the M2 workload execution under an
8\,Gb DRAM budget for both TeraHeap and FlexHeap. The Figure~\ref{fig:m2-th} subplot shows the TeraHeap run with static DRAM division, 
while \ref{fig:m2-fh} demonstrates the FlexHeap run. Figures~\ref{fig:m2-th-iow} \ref{fig:m2-fh-iow} show the corresponding I/O wait timelines accordingly.

The red line represents the DRAM allocated to the OS page cache, the blue line shows the
amount of used heap (H1) and in the case of TeraHeap, a flat purple line indicates the fixed heap capacity at 4\,Gb. 
This unchanging purple line in the TeraHeap plot visually confirms that no heap resizing takes place during the run. 

In the TeraHeap case, in the beginning we observe high GC activity suggesting the that the heap 
capacity is insufficient. This phase also coincides with increased I/O wait times, as presented in the 
iowait plot, indicating that data is being read from the H2 file while the page-cache has limited capacity,
resulting in evictions and increasing read traffic. As execution progresses, the iowait continues to spike,
while the heap gradually grows and then drops, implying that there is unused memory that could be 
assigned to the page-cache to reduce I/O.

In contrast, FlexHeap dynamically shrinks and expands the H1 heap based on workload phases. 
At the beginning of execution, the large I/O wait spikes observed in the TeraHeap run are no longer present, 
as FlexHeap performs heap shrinkages to allocate more DRAM to the page cache. 
The graph also reveals that heap expansions occur within the same phase, 
providing sufficient space to the heap.
This adaptability mitigates I/O bottlenecks but also reduces garbage collection pressure, 
as evidenced by a 63\% reduction in total GC time during the run.
As the benchmark continues, the used heap remains low, suggesting that the page-cache could 
benefit from shrink actions in order to utilize the unused memory. This is evident as the iowait
remains low throughout, compared to the TeraHeap run.

To evaluate FlexHeap, on Spark,  we run a diverse suite of workloads and compare its performance against TeraHeap. 
Figure~\ref{fig:spark-eval} presents a normalized execution time breakdown across seven Spark benchmarks. 
FlexHeap improves overall execution time in 5 out of 7 workloads \textit{PageRank}, \textit{ConnectedComponent},
\textit{ShortestPaths}, \textit{TriangleCount} and \textit{LogisticRegression} where it also reduces total garbage collection (GC) time and read traffic, 
on the device that contains the H2 file.

In contrast, benchmarks like \textit{SVDPlusPlus} and \textit{LinearRegression} report worse execution time compared to TeraHeap.
We analyze these cases in more detail in the following subsections to understand the underlying factors affecting FlexHeap’s effectiveness.

In contrast, the FlexHeap configuration (Figures~\ref{fig:triangle_flex_heap} and~\ref{fig:triangle_flex_iow}) 
demonstrates a more adaptive behavior. Heap shrinkages allow DRAM to be transferred to the page-cache leading to lower I/O wait. 
We observe that the peak I/O wait in ~\ref{fig:triangle_native_iow}, is reduced from 15\% down to 2\% in ~\ref{fig:triangle_flex_iow}. Notably, the prolonged high I/O wait phases observed in the TeraHeap run are effectively 
eliminated.


Figure~\ref{fig:svd_flexheap_combined} presents the behavior of \texttt{SVDPlusPlus} under FlexHeap.
We explain why it reports 10\% worse execution time and 33\% higher read traffic compared to the static configuration.

Subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_pagecache} shows the resizing of the primary heap. The blue line represents the used heap, the purple line shows 
the committed heap capacity, and the red line the page cache size. Subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_gc_io} illustrates the activity
between GC and I/O. GC time is plotted on the left Y-axis (green line), while I/O wait time is shown on the right Y-axis (red line), 
since the scale of I/O time is much smaller than that of GC time for easier visualization. Both share a
common X-axis (timestamp), allowing a direct comparison of their behavior.
Subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_iow}, shows the \%IOWait values. 


An analysis of the execution timeline indicates that FlexHeap overestimates GC pressure.
As shown in Subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_gc_io}, the estimated GC CPU time reaches up to 3{,}500 seconds
per interval. This arises from a high number of mutator threads, up to 183 which inflates 
the GC CPU time due to the current estimation method, given by \( \text{GC STW overhead} = \text{interval\_time} \times \min(\text{mutators}, \text{processors}) \).
Consequently, the resizing policy becomes 
biased against shrink actions, resulting in insufficient DRAM being allocated to the page cache. 
As illustrated in Subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_pagecache}, FlexHeap performs shrink actions only during major I/O peaks. 
However, the corresponding I/O wait subfigure~\ref{fig:svd_iow} indicates persistently high I/O wait times, due to  
the overestimation of GC time. As a result, the heap 
remains at a grown capacity for extended periods, limiting the available space for the page cache and leading to 
increased traffic.
